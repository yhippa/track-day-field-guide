Upper Novice → Intermediate

Track Day Field Guide

The concepts that matter most, synthesized from four foundational books

Going Faster — Skip Barber Speed Secrets — Ross Bentley Ultimate Speed Secrets — Ross Bentley Drive to Win — Carroll Smith
Section 01

The One Model That Explains Everything

Every technique in every one of these books is a consequence of one physical reality: a tire has a fixed budget of total traction force, and at any moment you're spending that budget on some combination of braking, cornering, and acceleration. Spend it all on braking and you can't turn. Spend it all on cornering and you can't accelerate. This budget is the Traction Circle.

The Principle

The orange line shows the ideal path through one corner: maximum braking that gradually transitions into cornering, then cornering that transitions into acceleration. The goal is to always be using all available traction — the circle is always full, just the direction changes. Any gap between what you're doing and the rim of the circle is wasted grip and wasted time.

This is why "brake, then turn, then accelerate" is wrong. All three overlap. Braking trails off as cornering builds. Cornering trails off as acceleration builds. The transitions are smooth arcs on the circle, not right angles.

What this means practically

Every rule you'll read below — trail braking, late apex, releasing the car on exit, early throttle — is just the traction circle applied to a specific moment in the corner. When you internalize the model, the individual rules stop being rules and start being obvious.

Section 02

The Four Phases of a Corner

Every corner, regardless of shape or speed, has the same structure. Carroll Smith defines it in Drive to Win and it maps directly onto the traction circle. Understand the phases and you stop thinking about corners as single events — you start seeing them as a sequence you can analyze and improve piece by piece.

01
Turn-In

Braking pressure is fading. You begin to steer. The car's nose must come down gradually — a sudden lift transfers weight rearward and kills front traction exactly when you need it. In fast corners, you almost wish the car in. In slow ones, you're more deliberate.

02
Turn-In → Throttle-On

The most important phase. How fast you can carry the car through this arc — while still arriving at the throttle-on point with the car balanced — determines your entire exit speed. Corner exit speed is set here, not at the apex.

03
Pure Cornering

A brief moment of maximum cornering force and neutral throttle — just enough gas to maintain speed. This isn't coasting. It's stabilizing the car's attitude before power goes on. It may only last a fraction of a second in a 90° corner.

04
Corner Exit

The payoff. Power goes on, steering unwinds, the car opens toward the exit. He who gets the power down first wins. Not he who carried the most speed in. Everything before this phase exists to make this phase as early and as clean as possible.

The Counterintuitive Truth

Most drivers focus on the apex — where to clip it, whether they hit it. But the apex is just a consequence of getting Phases 1 and 2 right. Stop fixating on the apex and start fixating on the quality of Phase 2: is the car balanced? Am I arriving at the throttle-on point smoothly, or am I still managing an entry I overcooked?

Section 03

The Fundamental Truths About Line

These are Carroll Smith's "Fundamental Truths" — the principles that govern where on the road you should be and why. They're not rules you memorize; they're consequences of physics that you eventually discover independently if you drive long enough. Better to just know them now.

  • 01 The late apex. For most corners, the ideal apex is past the geometric center of the corner. A late apex opens the exit radius and allows earlier power application — the most valuable thing in the corner. It also provides an insurance margin at exit. Downside: tighter Phase 2, slower apex speed, open door on the inside.
  • 02 Faster corner = earlier apex. In slow corners, you trade entry speed for an open exit because you can accelerate fast enough to make it worthwhile. In fast corners, you can't accelerate fast enough to recover the speed you sacrifice — so carry it through. The faster the corner, the earlier and rounder the apex, the smoother the inputs, the more you finesse rather than rotate.
  • 03 You must release the car on exit. After the apex, the steering wheel should only be unwinding. If you're adding lock after the apex, you turned in too early. Adding lock after the apex means understeer drag — you're fighting the car instead of accelerating. This is the most common error at every level below expert.
  • 04 Some entry understeer is correct. For the rear tires to have traction available for acceleration, they can't be at 100% of their cornering capacity during Phases 2 and 3. So fast cars push a little on entry. If you're fighting the push aggressively, you're sacrificing rear grip reserve. Corner-exit oversteer looks heroic and is slower than mild entry understeer with early throttle.
Section 04

What "The Limit" Actually Feels Like

This is Carroll Smith's slip-angle framework — and it's the clearest model any of the books offers for understanding where you are relative to the car's actual capability.

The tire's cornering force increases as slip angle increases, peaks, and then falls off. The shape of that curve is important: it's not a cliff, it's a rounded hill. There's a zone of maximum-ish performance on both sides of the peak. The question is which zone you're operating in.

Talent
Wide operating band, both sides of the peak. The driver waits for the tires to break loose (squeal, slide, snap) before reacting. Reacts to the limit rather than sensing it. Speed is inconsistent. This is where most HPDE drivers live, and there's nothing wrong with it — it's where learning happens.
Excellence
Operating just below peak slip angle, intentionally. ~95% of maximum cornering force, but the tire isn't being stressed. The driver can feel where the tires are before they protest. Consistent, precise, and easy on equipment. This is the target. It takes years to develop, not sessions.
Genius
Deliberately at or just past peak, in qualifying or a dice. Very narrow margin, very high reward. Where excellence-level drivers go on purpose for a single lap. Not sustainable for race distance. The line between excellence and genius is much thinner than the line between talent and excellence.
What This Means For You Now

The goal at your level is not to get to "Excellence" — it's to narrow the Talent band. Start feeling what the tires are doing before they protest rather than after. The feedback is there: steering weight changes, subtle vibrations, the way the car tracks. You're learning to read those signals earlier. That's the whole game. It takes seat time, not technique hacks.

The steering feedback signal

Carroll Smith notes that self-aligning torque peaks slightly before cornering force peaks. In plain terms: the steering gets lighter just before the tires hit their limit. If you wait for the steering to go completely light before backing off, you've already passed the cornering force peak. The goal is to work in the range where steering feel is still building — that's the Excellence zone. This is the feedback signal that becomes clearer with experience.

Section 05

Where to Look and How to Navigate

This section is underrated. All four books address it, Smith most directly, and it's one of the fastest ways to improve — entirely mental, no car required.

The Core Rule

Look where you want to go, not where you are. At the limit, you have almost no influence over where the car goes in the next 20 feet — so looking there is useless. Your brain processes what your eyes give it. If you're looking at the front bumper or the car in front of you, information arrives faster than your brain can use it. If you look far ahead, everything slows down and you have time to respond.

Reference points — the connect-the-dots system

Consciously or not, every driver navigates by reference points: fixed landmarks that trigger responses. A discolored patch of pavement that says "brake here." A shadow line at turn-in. The curb edge at the apex. A crack in the asphalt at track-out. Smith calls it connect-the-dots — you're linking points, not following a continuous line.

The more reference points, the smoother the driving. With few landmarks, the scene rushes at you in jerks. With many, it flows. Deliberately identify and name your reference points each session — especially in the corners where you feel vague or slow.

The visualization test

At home, try to mentally drive the circuit — smoothly, without hesitation, close to your actual lap time. Where do you stall or go vague? Those are the corners where your mental map is incomplete, which is almost always where you're losing time. Filling in reference points for those corners is one of the highest-return things you can do between sessions.

"If you cannot drive the track with your eyes closed — without hesitation and pretty darn close to your actual lap time — the chances are that you haven't picked up enough reference points." — Carroll Smith, Drive to Win

Where specifically to look at each point

On the straight approaching the braking zone: look to your braking marker
In the braking zone: look to your turn-in mark
At turn-in: look to the apex
At the apex: look to the exit / track-out point
At exit: look down the next straight

Eyes should always be one phase ahead of where the car is. If you're at turn-in and still looking at the braking marker, you're already behind.

Section 06

Smooth Inputs — What It Actually Means

Every book makes smoothness sound like an aesthetic preference. It isn't. It's physics. Ross Bentley in Speed Secrets makes the mechanical case clearly: any abrupt input causes a weight transfer spike that momentarily reduces tire contact and grip. Snap the wheel, stomp the brake, stab the throttle — in each case, you've just reduced your traction budget at the worst moment.

The Four Inputs — What Smooth Means For Each

Steering: Turn-in should be a smooth, progressive arc. No jabbing. In fast corners especially, any abruptness upsets the car's attitude when it's already on the limit. Practice "thinking" the car into the corner rather than physically cranking it in.

Braking: Squeeze to maximum, then progressively trail off. Don't stab the pedal. Don't release it suddenly. The release of the brakes at turn-in is the transition from braking force to cornering force on the traction circle — it should be a smooth arc, not a right angle.

Throttle: Roll it on. At corner exit, the transition from neutral throttle to full throttle should be progressive — fast, but progressive. The earlier and smoother you apply it, the more the car can use it without snapping into oversteer or pushing into understeer.

Gear changes: Smooth enough to be heard but not felt. A good downshift shouldn't upset the car's balance. A bad one can induce sudden oversteer at exactly the wrong time — when the car is already loaded in Phase 2.

"The hands and feet should feel like they belong to a pianist, not a boxer." — paraphrased from Speed Secrets, Ross Bentley

Smoothness is also a diagnostic tool

If you need to make a sudden corrective input — catch a slide, add lock to avoid running wide — that's information. Something earlier in the corner wasn't right. Smooth laps aren't just faster; they're telling you that Phases 1–3 were executed correctly. Busy laps tell you something was off at the entry.

Section 07

Trail Braking — Understand It First

Trail braking appears in all four books and is consistently identified as one of the last things a driver learns to do well. Here's the model without the mysticism.

What Trail Braking Is

Continuing to apply diminishing brake pressure into the corner — well past the turn-in point — rather than completing all braking in a straight line. The braking "trails off" as cornering builds. You're spending the traction budget on both simultaneously, riding the rim of the traction circle through the transition.

Why It Works

Trail braking keeps weight on the front tires (improving front traction and turn-in response) and reduces rear lateral traction (reducing entry understeer). It also allows you to brake slightly later because you're still slowing down while turning. It's not a hack — it's the physically correct way to "ride the rim" at corner entry.

When It Doesn't Work

In very fast corners — braking against partial throttle changes pitch attitude at a moment when the car is on a knife edge. In bumpy corners — modulation becomes unpredictable. When you're still learning — the footwork is complex and the margin for error is small.

For Your Level Right Now

Don't force it. Understand the model, feel for when you're unconsciously doing it already (you probably are in some corners), and let it develop naturally. Smith's exact advice: "While you are learning the technique, during the race, if in doubt don't. Slow in and fast out beats fast in and slow out."

Section 08

Most Common Mistakes at Your Level

These are the errors that appear consistently across all four books when describing the upper novice / lower intermediate driver. You don't need to go looking for these — they'll find you. The fix for each follows the same logic as the sections above.

Mistake

Early apex. Turning in too soon, clipping the inside of the corner early, then running out of track on exit and either lifting or running wide.

Fix

Delay the turn-in. Resist the urge. The corner will come to you. A later turn-in means a later apex and an exit that opens up rather than closes.

Mistake

Choking the exit. Adding or holding steering lock past the apex. The car understeers, you can't get on the throttle, and you're fighting the car to the end of the corner.

Fix

Almost always caused by an early apex. Fix the turn-in point and the exit opens automatically. After the apex, the wheel should only ever be unwinding.

Mistake

Looking at the wrong place. Eyes on the apex when you should be looking at exit. Eyes on the car ahead instead of the track. Feeling rushed and late everywhere.

Fix

Force your eyes one phase ahead. At turn-in, look for the apex. At apex, look for the exit. At exit, look down the straight. It feels wrong at first; it isn't.

Mistake

Braking too late instead of learning to brake later. Chasing the deepest possible braking point before the technique is consistent. Results in overcooked entries and time lost managing the aftermath.

Fix

Brake slightly early, manage the corner cleanly, and focus on exit. "When you brake a little early you lose very little time and have time to analyze the situation" (Smith). Late braking is the last thing to optimize, not the first.

Mistake

Driving at 10/10ths too soon. Pushing before the mental model is built. Variance is high, mistakes are repeated, learning slows down.

Fix

Drive at 8/10ths with conscious intent. Pick one thing to focus on per session. Consistency at 8/10ths builds the neural pathways that make 9/10ths feel like 7/10ths later.

Mistake

Throttle-then-steer on corner exit. Waiting until the car is fully straight to apply power. Losing most of the exit acceleration advantage.

Fix

Apply throttle as you begin unwinding the wheel. They overlap. Power goes on while you're still cornering — that's correct. The traction circle has room for both simultaneously on exit.

Section 09

Your Session-by-Session Progression

The worst way to improve is to chase everything at once. The books consistently recommend one conscious focus per session, letting everything else run on the level it's already at. Here's a suggested order of priority for where you are now.

1

Build Your Reference Points

Before optimizing any technique, build a dense mental map of the circuit. Identify a braking marker, turn-in mark, apex point, and exit point for every corner. The more specific, the better. A session spent on this will pay dividends for every session after it.

2

Practice Looking Ahead

Deliberately push your vision one phase ahead in every corner. This feels uncomfortable at first — trust it. You'll find the car goes where you're looking more than you realized.

3

Late Apex, Every Corner

Delay your turn-in. Feel the corner open up in front of you on exit instead of closing down. This single change fixes early apexes, exit understeer, and choking all at once. You'll probably feel like you're arriving at the apex too late — that's probably exactly right.

4

Corner Exit Throttle

Focus on getting the throttle on earlier and rolling it on more smoothly. Not harder — earlier. Pick the point where you want to apply throttle before the corner and commit to it, even if it feels too soon. Then evaluate the exit.

5

Smooth Brake Release

Focus on the quality of your brake release at turn-in. Progressive, not sudden. The nose should come down gradually as you steer in. This is what makes Phase 1 work and what sets up Phase 2.

6

Braking Depth (Last)

Only after the above are consistent: work on braking later. Not as the first thing you optimize — as the last. A lap of early braking done perfectly is faster than a lap of late braking done inconsistently.

Section 10

The Mental Side — What the Books Agree On

All four books converge on a few mental principles that don't get discussed enough in HPDE settings.

Subconscious Driving Is the Goal

Speed comes from getting technique to the level where it runs automatically — where you're not thinking about braking points and apex targets consciously, because the reference points are in your RAM and the responses are ballistic. The conscious mind then becomes available for reading the car, adapting to changing conditions, and managing the situation. You're not fast because you think faster. You're fast because you've stopped needing to think about certain things at all.

Visualization Is Training

Mental laps done vividly — with actual steering inputs, actual feelings, actual timing — lay down the same neural pathways as physical laps. Used correctly before a session or between runs, visualization activates what your body already knows and primes the right responses. The test: can you drive the circuit in your head without hesitation? If not, the gaps in your mental map are the gaps in your performance.

Consistency First, Speed Second

Bentley's formulation in Speed Secrets: drive at 8/10ths with precision and build from there. A driver who can hit the same reference points every lap at 8/10ths will improve faster than one who hits 10/10ths occasionally and gets different results every time. Consistency is the signal that technique is actually working. Variability is the signal that something is still being done consciously and imprecisely.

"After a certain amount of skill and experience has been attained, 98% of this business is mental." — Carroll Smith, Drive to Win
Quick Reference

Before You Go Out — One-Page Checklist

One focus per session. Pick one item from each category, not all of them.

Before the session

Visualize the lap: turn-in points, apex points, exit points. Where do you hesitate?
Decide on one technical focus for this session. Write it down.
Identify 2–3 corners where you'll specifically apply that focus.

During the session (in order of priority)

Eyes: look to the next phase, not the current one.
Turn-in: later than feels right. Let the corner come to you.
After the apex: is the wheel only unwinding? If not, why?
Throttle: are you rolling it on, or waiting until straight to apply?
Inputs: can you feel the transitions, or are they abrupt?

After the session

In which corners did your focus work? In which didn't it?
Was there any moment where corrective inputs were needed? What caused them?
What's one thing to carry into the next session?
